# Open data covers species, not cultivars

Public resources are enough to build step 1 (is this sorghum?) but not step 2 (which cultivar?). For step 1, **iNaturalist holds about 10.8k Sorghum bicolor observations and tens of thousands of look-alike observations**. These are phone-style photos, and **BioCLIP 2 (MIT licence)** can zero-shot label and filter them before distillation into MobileNetV3-Small. Step 2 splits in two. On the reasoning side, the **ICRISAT "Sorghum Characterization" table on Genesys (42,869 accessions, 22 traits, CC BY-SA 4.0)**, the **USDA GRIN descriptor counts**, the **IBPGR/ICRISAT 1993 and UPOV TG/122/4 descriptor standards** and **Tyrrell's 2019 naive Bayes identification-key method** give a sound, licensable base for conditional probability tables. On the image side, nothing public matches the target. **No public image dataset carries descriptor-trait labels** (glume colour, panicle compactness and shape, grain covering, lustre). The only large cultivar-labelled set, **Sorghum-100**, is overhead gantry imagery of 100 US cultivars with an unconfirmed licence. There is a second gap: the genebank tables describe germplasm accessions (IS numbers), not the named released cultivars farmers grow. So the team must collect its own descriptor-labelled phone photos and hand-build cultivar profiles for local releases. The rest of the pipeline can start from open data now.

## iNaturalist and BioCLIP 2 cover step 1, except millets

For "sorghum vs look-alikes", the most useful resource is iNaturalist research-grade observations. They are mostly phone photos of whole plants, panicles and field edges, which is what app users will take. Live counts at species rank show where data is rich and where it is thin. **Johnson grass (Sorghum halepense) has 37,595 observations, against 10,835 for Sorghum bicolor** ([iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa?q=Sorghum&rank=species&per_page=30)). Maize has 18,230 ([iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa/autocomplete?q=Zea%20mays&per_page=5)) and sugarcane 5,128 ([iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa?q=Saccharum%20officinarum&per_page=3)). The millets are thin: **pearl millet has 1,082 observations and finger millet only 229** ([iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa?q=Cenchrus%20americanus&per_page=3); [iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa?q=Eleusine%20coracana&per_page=3)). The same query returns tall andropogonoid grasses that make good hard negatives: Indiangrass (29,366), big bluestem (29,085) and thatching grass (5,769) ([iNat API](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/taxa?q=Sorghum&rank=species&per_page=30)). Sudangrass (S. × drummondii) did not appear at species rank, so it has no usable public class.

Licences are the catch. Each iNat photo carries its own licence, and many are CC BY-NC, so **a commercial app must filter to CC0 and CC BY photos** through the iNat API, iNat Open Data or a GBIF media download. The per-licence mix for sorghum photos was not measured. Expect label noise near the decision boundary too. Some "Sorghum bicolor" observations will be feral, ornamental or shattercane forms. Pl@ntNet lists roughly 1,600 to 2,000 Sorghum bicolor observations per regional flora ([Pl@ntNet](https://identify.plantnet.org/comores/species/Sorghum%20bicolor%20(L.)%20Moench/data)), but its API and commercial terms could not be read. **PlantNet-300K** (306,146 images, 1,081 species, per-image licences, BSD-2 code) skews European, and nobody has yet checked whether its class list includes sorghum, maize or Johnson grass ([GitHub](https://github.com/plantnet/PlantNet-300K)). A grep of `plantnet300K_species_id_2_name.json` settles that.

**BioCLIP 2** is the practical teacher model. It is MIT-licensed, ViT-L/14, trained on about 214M images across 952K taxa, and does zero-shot classification from species names ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/imageomics/bioclip-2)). It is far too large for a phone. Its job is offline: prompt it with Sorghum bicolor, Zea mays, Cenchrus americanus, Eleusine coracana, Saccharum officinarum, Sorghum halepense and S. × drummondii to clean and pseudo-label scraped photos, then distil it into the MobileNetV3-Small species head. The MIT tag permits commercial use. Its LAION-2B base and the mixed licences of its training images still leave a provenance question, which the team should note but which does not block use. The PlantNet-300K ResNet18 weights carry no stated licence ([GitHub](https://github.com/plantnet/PlantNet-300K)).

The weak point of step 1 is not data volume but validation. **No paper was found that classifies sorghum against maize, millet, sugarcane or Johnson grass from RGB photos**, so no published accuracy exists to beat. One study found that sorghum-trained organ segmentation transferred well to maize leaves and stalks but poorly to maize reproductive organs ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10529619)). That means vegetative parts look alike to models, and **the panicle is the most discriminative view**. The app should ask for a head photo whenever the plant is at heading.

([SorghumWeed paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10789999/); [CropAndWeed labels](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cropandweed/cropandweed-dataset/main/cnw/utilities/datasets.py); [GrainSet paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632488/))

**GrainSet** is the biggest open sorghum image source of any kind. Its kernels are imaged on a vibration belt under shadow-free circular lighting ([PMC10632488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632488/)). That looks nothing like a handful of grain on a palm. It needs heavy augmentation (backgrounds, blur, multi-kernel composites) before it helps a phone model. GrainSpace (5.25M images) has no sorghum ([arXiv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.05306)). **No open millet grain dataset and no labelled pearl- or finger-millet plant dataset were found.** Millet negatives therefore have to come from iNat's small pool plus the team's own photos, for plants and for grain alike. The UQ panicle boxes are top-down canopy views, so crops from them should be mixed with side-on iNat panicles rather than used alone.

## No public image set reads descriptor traits

Step 2 needs images labelled with IBPGR/ICRISAT descriptor states, or failing that, with cultivars. The search found **no public image dataset labelled for panicle compactness, panicle shape, glume colour, grain covering, awns, lustre or midrib colour**. Every cultivar-labelled set is either the wrong viewpoint or not downloadable.

The benchmark numbers show how hard fine-grained cultivar ID is even in the best case. **Sorghum-100 tops out at 78.8% test accuracy across 100 cultivars**, under a cross-plot split, with whole-image pooling at 72.1% ([ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.05748)). For seeds, **an RGB-only ResNet50 reached 86.6% on 8 varieties**, rising to 94.8% only with hyperspectral fusion ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12412220/)). That is a realistic ceiling for phone grain photos of a small local variety set. The nearest thing to a learned descriptor trait is Zhang 2022, where CNNs predicted leaf-wax and tan alleles from plot images at **0.65–0.83 per-cultivar accuracy against 0.5 chance** ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2022.872858/full)). Plot images carry this kind of signal, but only weakly.

**Santiago et al. 2024 is the closest published analogue to the app.** It used a 64 MP phone held about 1 m from the panicle, with other plants behind, and got **YOLOv8-seg panicle segmentation at AP50 ≈ 0.89** ([PMC11658820](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11658820/)). That figure is a reasonable target for the panicle-crop stage that precedes trait reading. Its data is on request and its code is on GitHub, but whether trained weights are included was not checked. The reusable sorghum model artefacts (DeepSorghumHead RetinaNet weights, the SegVoteNet 3D checkpoint, the Sorghum_Grain_Counter code) **all lack a licence** ([DeepSorghumHead](https://github.com/oceam/DeepSorghumHead); [SegVoteNet](https://github.com/chr15-j/SegVoteNet)). By default they are all-rights-reserved, so the authors must give permission before anything ships. Grain colour and size probably do not need a learned model at all. A 2024 study found that off-the-shelf seed-segmentation models beat human visual colour scoring in GWAS power ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.07.22.604683v1)). That supports the project's "measured" trait path for grain, though the study's data availability was not verified.

## Genebank tables and Tyrrell's method make naive Bayes feasible

The non-image side is the strongest part of the evidence. **The ICRISAT "Sorghum Characterization" dataset gives 42,869 accessions scored on 22 traits** at Patancheru from 1973 to 2024, as xlsx under **CC BY-SA 4.0** ([Genesys](https://www.genesys-pgr.org/datasets/fbb7bb31-67bb-4010-9fe1-5bf7ac55adc8)). The traits are race, plant pigmentation, midrib colour, panicle exsertion, length, width, compactness and shape, glume colour and covering, seed colour, lustre, subcoat and size, 100-seed weight, endosperm texture and threshability. Most of them are phone-visible. CC BY-SA allows commercial use, but **any derived CPT file shipped in the app must itself be CC BY-SA with attribution**. That matches the project's plan to carry the attribution in `cultivars-vN.json`. **USDA GRIN** adds per-value accession counts across 13 study environments, for example GLUMECOLOR: Purple 5,849, Black 3,176, Straw 1,739, Red 1,362, plus Mixed (837) and Segregating (739) codes ([NPGS descriptor 69042](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/descriptordetail?id=69042)). The Mixed and Segregating counts and the multi-environment scoring are exactly what the team needs to estimate within-accession variability and set the noise floor. No data licence was stated; as US-government data it is probably public domain, but that is an inference. **KALRO's Kibos 2022 dataset** (193 accessions, 17 traits including midrib, glume and grain colour) is CC BY-NC 4.0, so it cannot go into a commercial app ([Genesys](https://www.genesys-pgr.org/datasets/cde06d9c-671b-4f20-ac60-f37af35274fd)).

The state vocabularies come from two standards that disagree in detail. The **IBPGR/ICRISAT 1993 list on Genesys has 40 descriptors**. It gives 8 glume-colour states, 6 grain-colour states and 13 inflorescence compactness-and-shape states, from "very lax" to "broom corn" ([Genesys descriptor list](https://www.genesys-pgr.org/descriptorlists/6291a6d8-d034-4c16-b302-59f9e92b9cdc)). The full PDF manual exists ([ICRISAT OAR](https://oar.icrisat.org/1011/1/RA_00246.pdf)), but it could not be fetched, so its "highly discriminating" subset is unverified. **UPOV TG/122/4 has 36 characteristics, about 25 of them visual**. Its glume colour at maturity has 7 states and its grain colour 11 ([TG/122/4](https://coboru.gov.pl/Publikacje_coboru/Metodyki/UPOV/TG1224%20z%2025.03.2015.pdf)). It also separates glume colour at flowering from glume colour at maturity. **Growth stage changes the observed state**, so the app must either record the stage or restrict itself to maturity traits. With 7 (UPOV), 8 (IBPGR) and 9 (GRIN) glume-colour codes in play, the team needs a crosswalk to a coarser phone-observable set (for example white/straw, yellow/tan, red/brown, purple/black) before building CPTs.

The reasoning method already exists in open form. **Tyrrell (2019) describes a naive Bayes identification key** with Gaussian likelihoods for continuous characters, frequency-weighted probability tables for discrete states, additive smoothing against zero probabilities, and an evidence score in decibels. It is CC BY 4.0 with R code ([PMC6526653](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6526653)). The author warns about correlated characters, and his own test cases produced ties. This supports the project rule of returning groups and "unrecognized" instead of forcing an argmax. UPOV's practice for near-identical varieties points the same way. It sorts candidates by **8 grouping characteristics** (panicle emergence time, stigma colour, self-fertility, plant length, panicle density at maturity, position of broadest part, grain colour, photoperiod sensitivity) before any side-by-side comparison ([TG/122/4](https://coboru.gov.pl/Publikacje_coboru/Metodyki/UPOV/TG1224%20z%2025.03.2015.pdf)). Two of these, panicle density and grain colour, are phone-readable, which makes them a natural coarse first stage. Field evidence favours multiple views. **PlantVillage Nuru's cassava accuracy rose from single-leaf rates as low as 21–59% to 73–93% when 6 leaves per plant were combined** ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2020.590889/full)). In naive Bayes terms, that means multiplying likelihoods across several panicles or grain samples. No concept-bottleneck model has been applied to plant ID, and no published Bayesian sorghum cultivar identifier was found, so this pipeline has no direct precedent.

For colour, **PlantCV's `correct_color` uses a 24-patch ColorChecker** to fit a correction matrix ([PlantCV docs](https://docs.plantcv.org/en/latest/transform_correct_color/)). Smartphone studies report R² > 0.85 against a chromameter after calibration ([Jurnal UGM](https://journal.ugm.ac.id/jbp/article/download/75631/37205)). That supports mapping calibrated CIELAB values to coarse descriptor colour classes. No ΔE figure for phones on plant tissue was found.

**The genebank tables do not describe the cultivars farmers grow.** Named released varieties appear only in semi-structured national sources. IIMR's PDF of nationally released kharif varieties gives free-text "salient features" such as "Glumes straw coloured. Grain white pearly" for CSV 3, but coverage thins to "compact head" for later releases ([IIMR PDF](https://millets.res.in/technologies/kharif_sorghum_varieties.pdf)). Ethiopia's seed portal records mostly seed colour, often blank ([ethioseed](https://ethioseed.moa.gov.et/seed-variety-details/1031/)). India's DUS data (33 characteristics, 117 candidate varieties, more than 110 reference varieties) is held by PPV&FRA and is not public ([IIMR DUS](https://www.millets.res.in/dus.php)). No open UPOV, CPVO, Kenyan, Nigerian or Sudanese per-variety tables turned up.

## Gaps the team must fill, and where to start

Five gaps decide the project's data plan. First, **descriptor-labelled phone photos of leaves, panicles and grain** do not exist publicly. Without them, no learned trait head can be trained or calibrated, and per-head ECE cannot be measured. Second, **cultivar profiles for named local releases** must be built by hand, from IIMR or national-register text, from accession cross-references where a release has an IS number, or best from the team's own side-by-side scoring of seed-lot samples. Third, **millet and sudangrass negatives** (plant and grain) are too thin in public data. Fourth, **a step-1 validation set** of real field photos is needed, because no published sorghum-vs-look-alike benchmark exists. Fifth, **licence clearance** is still open for Sorghum-100, GRIN, the unlicensed model repositories and BioCLIP 2's training provenance.

The recommended starting set, all usable in a commercial app unless noted, is as follows.

Two requests are worth sending at once. Ask the Santiago 2024 authors for their phone panicle images and YOLOv8-seg weights. Ask the Yang 2025 authors for their seed images. Both are on request, and both are the nearest public analogues to the app's panicle and grain views.

## Conclusion

The project's architecture fits the evidence better than an end-to-end cultivar classifier would. Open data cannot train a model that maps photos to named cultivars. The best public cultivar benchmark, Sorghum-100, reaches only 79% with ideal same-season imagery, and nothing public covers the target viewpoint. Open data can supply most of what a trait-then-Bayes pipeline needs: a species gate, colour calibration, state vocabularies, accession-level trait frequencies and a published inference method. The scarce asset is therefore a modest, carefully labelled set of phone photos scored on perhaps eight to ten maturity-stage descriptors. Pooling across cultivars, as trait heads do, makes far better use of labelling effort than per-cultivar classes would.

The harder unknown is not vision but identifiability. Many released cultivars may share a coarse phone-observable profile, especially once glume and grain colours are collapsed into four or five classes. The team should test this early, on paper, before collecting any images. Encode the target cultivar list's descriptor profiles, count how many fall into indistinguishable groups, and use the result to decide which traits are worth learning and when the app should honestly answer with a group.
